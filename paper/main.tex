\begin{abstract}
Text de-identification is often evaluated as a span removal problem: did a
system remove names, dates, locations, contact information, and other explicit
identifiers?  For sensitive text used in foundation-model workflows, this is
necessary but incomplete.  A transformed clinical vignette or legal case excerpt
can pass a privacy-oriented screen while removing the medication dose, lab value,
statutory article, procedural posture, or legal claim needed for downstream
reasoning.  We study this failure mode through a controlled clinical/legal
benchmark for utility-preserving anonymization.  The benchmark pairs privacy
labels with task-critical facts and downstream QA items.  We introduce
\textit{Task-Critical Fact Retention} (\tcfr), a fact-level utility metric, and
\method, a low-cost protocol that extracts privacy spans and important domain
facts, anonymizes under preservation constraints, verifies the result, and
applies deterministic safety/repair rules.  In a 150-example non-oracle LLM run,
\method{} attains zero measured direct identifier leaks, lower quasi-identifier
risk than both baselines, and the best audited fact retention.  Additional
GPT-5.5 audits and diagnostic stress cases support the same qualitative lesson:
``redact more'' is not a general solution, because some sensitive facts are also
the task-critical answer.  The results support evaluating de-identification
systems by both privacy protection and retained domain meaning.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Sensitive text is increasingly important in foundation-model workflows.  Clinical
notes, legal decisions, support logs, and other restricted corpora can support
retrieval-augmented generation, supervised fine-tuning, evaluation, safety
auditing, and synthetic-data generation \cite{bommasani2021foundation,lewis2020rag}.
Clinical resources such as MIMIC-IV-Note illustrate both the scientific value of
free-text records and the operational burden of de-identification before
research access \cite{johnson2023mimicnote}.  Legal corpora raise a parallel
problem: case narratives may contain names, application numbers, dates,
locations, party roles, confidential attributes, and statutory details, while the
same documents are valuable for legal QA and reasoning benchmarks
\cite{pilan2022tab}.  The core promise of responsible data transformation is to
unlock such data while reducing privacy risk.

Most practical de-identification pipelines begin with a reasonable objective:
remove or mask personally identifying information.  This objective is reflected
in regulatory and operational guidance such as HIPAA de-identification guidance
\cite{hhsdeid}, the GDPR distinction between personal data, pseudonymization, and
anonymization \cite{gdpr}, and widely used tools such as Microsoft Presidio
\cite{presidio}.  These resources are essential, but they do not by themselves
answer the question that matters for model development: after transformation,
can the data still support the task for which it was retained?

The answer is not obvious.  In a clinical note, replacing ``methotrexate 15 mg
weekly'' with ``a medication'' may look privacy-conservative, yet it removes the
answer to a treatment question.  In a legal excerpt, replacing ``Article 6'' with
``a specified article'' removes the legal basis needed for downstream reasoning.
These are not cosmetic edits.  They change the meaning that a retrieval system,
model evaluator, or downstream annotator is expected to use.  Conversely,
preserving every useful clinical or legal fact can leave quasi-identifiers, rare
events, sensitive attributes, or combinations of details that support linkage or
re-identification.  Classical privacy work shows that quasi-identifiers can be
linkable even when names are removed \cite{sweeney2002kanonymity,narayanan2008robust},
and recent work shows that large language models can infer personal attributes
from ordinary text \cite{staab2023beyond}.

This paper studies the boundary between privacy removal and meaning preservation.
Our central question is:
\begin{quote}
\emph{When text is de-identified for foundation-model use, does the transformed
text still contain the facts needed for the intended clinical or legal task?}
\end{quote}
We focus on clinical-style vignettes and TAB-derived legal snippets because they
make the privacy--utility conflict concrete.  The important facts are not merely
surface tokens: they are medications, doses, lab values, diagnoses, legal
articles, claims, procedures, outcomes, and reasoning.  A useful anonymizer must
handle four cases differently: direct identifiers that should be removed, quasi-
identifiers that should often be generalized, task-critical facts that should be
preserved, and overlap facts that are both useful and privacy-relevant.

We make four contributions.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item We construct a controlled benchmark with synthetic clinical vignettes and
  TAB-derived legal snippets, each annotated with direct identifiers, quasi-
  identifiers, task-critical facts, and downstream QA items.
  \item We introduce \textit{Task-Critical Fact Retention} (\tcfr), a fact-level
  utility metric that distinguishes retained domain meaning from generic surface
  similarity.
  \item We propose \method, a simple low-cost anonymization protocol that makes
  the privacy--utility boundary explicit by extracting privacy spans and
  task-critical facts before transformation, then applying deterministic safety
  and repair checks.
  \item We provide an empirical audit showing a clear qualitative frontier:
  generic prompting preserves many facts but leaks identifiers; privacy-first
  prompting removes identifiers but often removes the answer; task-aware
  anonymization improves the observed privacy--utility tradeoff while surfacing
  residual policy-sensitive cases.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Clinical de-identification.}
Clinical de-identification has a long history in shared tasks and patient-note
benchmarks.  i2b2/UTHealth and related challenges framed the problem as detecting
and removing protected-health-information spans from longitudinal clinical
narratives \cite{stubbs2015automated}.  Neural systems later treated the task as
sequence labeling over PHI spans \cite{dernoncourt2016patientnotes}.  These
benchmarks and tools are important, but span recall does not directly measure
whether clinically material facts such as medications, doses, lab values,
procedures, diagnoses, and temporal relations remain usable after transformation.
MIMIC-IV-Note further shows the importance and difficulty of making clinical text
available: it contains de-identified clinical notes, but access remains
credentialed and its release required substantial de-identification work
\cite{johnson2023mimicnote}.

\paragraph{Legal anonymization.}
Legal anonymization differs from generic named-entity removal because legal
meaning is often carried by facts that look identifying: jurisdiction, statute,
case posture, party role, dates, and procedural history.  TAB provides an
important benchmark for legal anonymization by annotating European Court of Human
Rights cases with entity spans, masking decisions, confidential attributes, and
coreference relations \cite{pilan2022tab}.  Subsequent work on legal text
anonymization highlights that legal documents require privacy decisions that are
sensitive to document structure and legal meaning \cite{papadopoulou2023neural}.
Our legal split builds on TAB but adds a utility target: the legal article,
claim, outcome, and procedure should remain answerable after anonymization.

\paragraph{Privacy risk beyond direct identifiers.}
Removing direct identifiers does not guarantee anonymity.  Classical work on
k-anonymity and dataset de-anonymization shows that combinations of non-name
attributes can support linkage \cite{sweeney2002kanonymity,narayanan2008robust}.
Differential privacy provides formal guarantees for aggregate analysis and
learning algorithms \cite{dwork2006calibrating}, but most practical text
anonymization pipelines do not provide such guarantees.  Large language models
add a new practical concern: even when obvious identifiers are absent, models can
infer attributes from context \cite{staab2023beyond}.  Recent anonymization
benchmarks therefore increasingly evaluate residual re-identification risk and
utility jointly \cite{loiseau2025taueval,krco2026ratbench}, and adaptive
anonymization work treats the privacy--utility balance as a task-dependent
optimization problem \cite{loiseau2026adaptive}.  We share this motivation but
make the utility target explicit at the level of domain facts and QA answers.

\paragraph{Utility and faithfulness metrics.}
Generic text-generation metrics such as token overlap, embedding similarity, or
BERTScore \cite{bertscore} can be useful diagnostics, but they do not encode
which domain facts must be preserved.  High similarity can be bad if identifiers
remain; low similarity can be acceptable if the transformed text safely
paraphrases the original while preserving the answer.  This motivates evaluating
privacy-safe utility through task-critical facts rather than surface similarity
alone.  Our work is also related to broader calls for better dataset
documentation and explicit reporting of intended use and limitations
\cite{gebru2021datasheets,bender2018datastatements}.

\section{Benchmark and Task}

\paragraph{What counts as a good transformation?}
Given an input text $x$, a de-identification method outputs transformed text
$\hat{x}$.  Each example contains direct identifiers $D$, quasi-identifiers $Q$,
task-critical facts $F$, and QA items $A$.  The desired transformation should
remove direct identifiers, reduce linkability from quasi-identifiers, and retain
the facts needed to answer the domain questions.  This formulation separates
three notions that are often conflated:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item \textbf{Privacy removal}: names, IDs, exact dates, contact details, and
  case or record numbers should not remain.
  \item \textbf{Risk reduction}: rare or linkable context should be generalized
  when possible.
  \item \textbf{Task preservation}: medications, doses, labs, diagnoses,
  statutes, claims, outcomes, and procedural facts should remain when they are
  needed for the intended task.
\end{enumerate}
The hard cases occur when a fact belongs to both the second and third categories.
For example, a pregnancy status, domestic-violence claim, jurisdiction-specific
statute, or rare diagnosis may be both sensitive and central to the task.

\paragraph{Clinical split.}
We generate 75 synthetic clinical vignettes from templates.  Each vignette
contains direct identifiers such as patient name, clinician name, clinic, date,
medical record number, phone number, and email; quasi-identifiers such as age,
city, occupation, and family context; and task-critical facts such as diagnosis,
medication, dose, lab result, symptom duration, imaging result, or treatment
plan.  The split is synthetic by design: it provides controlled utility labels
without exposing real protected health information.  It should be read as a
controlled stress test, not as evidence of performance on real clinical notes.

\paragraph{Legal split.}
We sample 75 short excerpts from TAB, an open-source corpus of English-language
European Court of Human Rights cases annotated with personal identifiers, masking
decisions, confidential attributes, and coreference relations \cite{pilan2022tab}.
For each sampled snippet, we keep short procedure, complaint, and outcome
sentences when available.  We use TAB spans for privacy labels and heuristic
extraction plus manual spot checks for legal articles, claims, outcomes, and QA
answers.  This lets us study legal meaning without processing private legal
files.

\paragraph{Stress and robustness slices.}
We also construct a 12-example handwritten stress slice.  These examples place
privacy and utility in explicit conflict: pregnancy, sexual orientation,
domestic violence, Roma marriage, exact ages, rare occupations, case labels,
secret evidence, legal article/protocol specificity, numeric labs, and dose
preservation.  The stress slice is diagnostic rather than a headline benchmark;
it helps us interpret cases where the correct transformation depends on release
policy.

\section{Metrics}

We report privacy and utility metrics together because either side alone can be
misleading.

\paragraph{Privacy.}
\textit{Direct leak} is the fraction of outputs where any gold direct identifier
remains.  \textit{Direct span recall}, \textit{quasi span recall}, and
\textit{all-span recall} measure the fraction of annotated privacy spans no
longer found verbatim after transformation.  \textit{QI risk} is a row-level
audit score for retained quasi-identifiers such as specific locations, rare
occupations, exact dates, sensitive attributes, family relations, statute or
jurisdiction details, and unusual combinations of details.  The QI score is an
audit signal, not a formal anonymity guarantee.

\paragraph{Task-critical utility.}
For each example, let $F$ be the set of task-critical facts.  Exact
Task-Critical Fact Retention is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{TCFR}(\hat{x}) = \frac{1}{|F|}\sum_{f \in F} \mathbb{1}[f \text{ is retained in } \hat{x}].
\end{equation}
Exact matching is reproducible but conservative because faithful paraphrases may
not match the gold string.  We therefore also report audited \tcfr{} using a
structured LLM fact judge that marks each fact as preserved,
generalized-but-acceptable, omitted, contradicted, or unclear.  The judge is
strict for medication names, doses, lab values, legal Article numbers, outcomes,
and legal claims.  \textit{QA consistency} asks whether downstream questions can
still recover the gold answers from the anonymized text.

\paragraph{Diagnostic surface metrics.}
We compute token F1, TF-IDF cosine, and edit rate as diagnostic foils.  They are
not headline utility metrics: a high-similarity output may retain private
information, and a low-similarity output may still preserve the task answer.

\section{Methods}

We compare three non-oracle LLM anonymization protocols in the promoted
150-example run.  All methods operate on the same inputs and are evaluated with
the same gold privacy and utility annotations.

\paragraph{Generic LLM.}
The generic baseline asks the model to remove personally identifying information
while preserving document meaning.  It resembles a common one-shot instruction a
data steward might give to a general-purpose model.  This baseline tests whether
broad de-identification instructions are sufficient.

\paragraph{Privacy-first LLM.}
The privacy-first baseline asks the model to remove or generalize any information
that could identify a person, including rare locations, exact dates, named
institutions, rare occupations, unique events, and combinations of details.  When
uncertain, the prompt resolves the conflict by generalizing or deleting.  This
baseline tests the cost of maximizing apparent privacy without an explicit
utility target.

\paragraph{\method.}
\method{} is a structured protocol rather than a new model.  It has four stages.
First, an extraction prompt identifies privacy spans and task-critical domain
facts.  Privacy spans are labeled as direct identifiers or quasi-identifiers;
critical facts are labeled with whether generalization is allowed.  Second, a
constrained anonymization prompt receives these structures and is instructed to
replace direct identifiers with typed placeholders, generalize quasi-identifiers
when possible, preserve task-critical facts, and use the least identifying form
of facts that are both privacy-relevant and task-critical.  Third, a
verification/repair prompt checks for leaked direct identifiers, generalizable
quasi-identifiers, omitted facts, contradictions, and invented facts.  Finally, a
deterministic safety/repair layer replaces high-precision identifiers such as
case/application numbers, dates, MRNs, phone numbers, emails, names, and
institutions, and repairs known domain mistakes such as legal Article
over-generalization and medication placeholdering.

The method is intentionally simple.  The claim is not that LLM prompting solves
anonymization.  The claim is that de-identification improves when the prompt and
post-processing pipeline are organized around the privacy--utility boundary
rather than around a single instruction to ``remove PII.''

\paragraph{Diagnostic baselines.}
We additionally run local diagnostic baselines on a 200-example expansion and on
the stress slice: regex rules, Presidio, direct-span oracle replacement,
privacy-first oracle redaction, and a CSG oracle using gold benchmark
annotations.  Oracle variants are not deployable systems; they diagnose upper
bounds and failure modes.

\section{Experimental Setup}

The promoted non-oracle run contains 150 examples: 75 synthetic clinical
vignettes and 75 TAB-derived legal snippets.  The LLM methods are run with a GPT
model under a bounded API budget.  We use no real protected health information:
clinical examples are synthetic, and legal examples are public TAB-derived
snippets.  We compute deterministic privacy and exact-utility metrics from gold
spans and facts, then run an LLM-assisted fact-retention audit over the
anonymized outputs.  Paired deltas are computed over the same examples to compare
\method{} against each baseline.

We also ran three follow-up checks after the initial submission draft.  First, a
300-example sample-size diagnostic added 75 clinical and 75 legal rows beyond the
promoted n150 run.  Second, a GPT-5.5 fact-retention audit re-judged all
promoted n150 outputs to test judge dependence.  Third, a GPT-5.5 adversarial
privacy audit examined the 20 hardest rows.  These follow-ups are reported as
robustness checks, not as replacements for the main benchmark.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Results at a glance}

The results are easiest to read as three takeaways.

\textbf{Takeaway 1: generic de-identification keeps useful facts but leaks
privacy-relevant details.}  Generic prompting preserves many clinical and legal
facts, but it leaves direct identifiers in 27 of 150 examples and has the highest
QI risk.

\textbf{Takeaway 2: privacy-first de-identification removes identifiers but often
removes the answer.}  Privacy-first prompting reaches zero measured direct leaks,
but its exact \tcfr{} and QA consistency collapse because it frequently replaces
medications, doses, labs, diagnoses, legal articles, and claims with vague
placeholders.

\textbf{Takeaway 3: task-aware structure gives a better frontier, but does not
eliminate policy-sensitive residual cases.}  \method{} reaches zero measured
direct leaks, the lowest overall QI risk in the main run, and the best audited
\tcfr{} and QA consistency.  The remaining failures are concentrated in legal
cases where statute, jurisdiction, or sensitive-attribute specificity overlaps
with the task itself.

risk by deleting jurisdictional or statutory specificity, but that same
specificity is often needed to answer the legal question.  Thus, the legal subset
is where \method{} most clearly exposes policy-sensitive overlap rather than
simple anonymization errors.

\subsection{Follow-up audits support the same qualitative story}

The follow-up experiments are useful because they reduce two likely reviewer
concerns: sample-size dependence and judge dependence.  In the 300-example
sample-size diagnostic, the qualitative frontier persists: \method{} has zero
measured direct leaks, QI risk 0.21, and audited \tcfr{} 0.99, compared with
higher privacy risk for generic prompting and much lower utility for
privacy-first prompting.  We keep n150 as the promoted headline because the
manual-audit and analysis package is anchored there; n300 is robustness evidence.

The external GPT-5.5 fact-retention audit is stricter than the original low-cost
judge, but it preserves the utility ordering.  It scores audited \tcfr{} as 0.92
for \method{}, 0.90 for generic prompting, and 0.41 for privacy-first prompting.
This result is intentionally interpreted conservatively: the \method{}--generic
utility difference under GPT-5.5 is small, but generic still carries much higher
measured direct leakage and QI risk.  The GPT-5.5 adversarial privacy audit on
the 20 hardest rows similarly flags generic prompting more often than \method{},
while privacy-first remains safest by privacy flags but worst by task utility.

Table~\ref{tab:ablation} is important for claim discipline.  The verification

privacy gain comes from deterministic safety/repair, which closes eleven direct

high-precision identifiers such as IDs, dates, phone numbers, MRNs, emails, and
case/application numbers should still be checked deterministically.

\subsection{Surface similarity hides both privacy and utility failures}

Generic prompting and \method{} have nearly identical token F1 against the
original text, and generic prompting has higher TF-IDF similarity.  If we used
surface similarity as the utility metric, generic prompting would look
competitive or better.  But among high-similarity outputs, many still contain
privacy failures or audited fact losses.  This is why the paper uses fact-level
\tcfr{} and QA consistency as the primary utility measures.  Surface similarity
is a useful diagnostic, not a privacy-safe utility metric.

\subsection{Diagnostic baselines and stress cases}

The local/oracle diagnostics reinforce the same interpretation without adding a
large table to the main narrative.  Regex and direct-span replacement preserve
almost all exact task facts but leave high QI risk.  Presidio is useful
infrastructure but is not tailored to our mixed clinical/legal benchmark: it
misses many legal identifiers and sometimes removes clinical facts under exact
matching.  The privacy-first oracle nearly eliminates measured QI risk but loses
more than half of task-critical facts.  The CSG oracle shows that a gold-informed
task-aware policy can preserve utility while reducing QI risk, but it is an
upper-bound diagnostic rather than a deployable system.

The handwritten stress slice makes the limitation intuitive.  Privacy-first
oracle redaction drives measured QI risk to zero but fails QA on 11 of 12 rows.
Direct-span replacement preserves utility but leaves quasi-identifiers in every
row.  CSG oracle replacement preserves all task facts with zero direct leaks, but
retains task-critical quasi-identifiers in half of the rows.  These are not
ordinary implementation errors: in some examples, pregnancy, sexual orientation,
domestic violence, minority status, or exact legal basis is both sensitive and
necessary to answer the domain question.

\section{Qualitative Analysis}

\paragraph{Clinical over-generalization.}
In \texttt{clinical\_0006}, the original vignette describes rheumatoid arthritis
with MCP joint swelling, positive anti-CCP, and methotrexate 15 mg weekly plus
folic acid 1 mg daily.  The privacy-first output replaces these with ``finger
joint areas,'' ``a specific antibody test,'' ``an autoimmune joint condition,''
and ``a disease-modifying medication,'' so both clinical QA questions fail.
\method{} redacts the patient, clinic, date, MRN, phone, email, clinician, city,
and exact age while preserving the lab, diagnosis, medication, and doses.

\paragraph{Legal Article preservation.}
In \texttt{legal\_0006}, generic prompting leaves application number
\texttt{37645/97} and the exact filing date while preserving Article 6.  The
privacy-first output removes those identifiers but replaces Article 6 with ``a
specified article,'' breaking the legal-basis question.  \method{} removes the
application number, name, country, institution, nationality, and date while
preserving Article 6 and the reasonable-time civil-proceedings claim.

\paragraph{Residual frontier cases.}
The remaining frontier is visible in \texttt{legal\_0005}: generic and
privacy-first prompting avoid measured QI hits by generalizing away the specific
section 42 Fair Employment claim, while \method{} preserves Article 6 and the
exact claim.  The preserved statute name still contains ``Northern Ireland'' and
``1976,'' so the row remains a measured QI-risk case.  This is a useful
limitation.  Some legal statute, jurisdiction, regime, or offense specificity may
be both important for legal reasoning and quasi-identifying.  A residual audit of
the eleven remaining CSG QI rows found six clear task-critical legal-overlap
cases and five additional frontier candidates.

These examples motivate a policy distinction that span-level metrics cannot make
alone.  Some outputs are clearly bad because they leak direct identifiers.  Some
are clearly bad because they remove the answer.  Others are frontier cases where
the correct decision depends on the intended release setting.  A private internal
evaluation corpus may preserve more legal specificity than a public release.  A
public synthetic-data release may need to generalize facts that are otherwise
useful.  Our metric does not resolve these policy choices, but it makes them
visible.

\section{Limitations and Ethics}

This is a bounded evaluation study, not a formal anonymization guarantee.  The
clinical split is synthetic.  The legal split is public TAB-derived text, but
legal fact and claim extraction is heuristic and manually spot-checked rather
than fully expert annotated.  Audited \tcfr{} uses an LLM judge; it is useful for
separating paraphrase from omission, but independent human annotations remain
important for final claims.  The privacy metrics detect known direct spans and
heuristic quasi-identifiers; they do not provide k-anonymity, differential
privacy, legal compliance, or protection against a motivated adversary with
external knowledge.

We do not release or process real protected health information.  The intended use
of this work is evaluation and safer data transformation research.  The main
ethical lesson is that aggressive redaction can make sensitive data safer to
share only by making it useless or misleading.  Responsible release pipelines
should therefore state the downstream utility requirements explicitly, document
residual risk, and audit both privacy leakage and task-critical meaning loss
before using transformed text in foundation-model workflows.

\section{Conclusion}

De-identification systems should be evaluated not only by what they remove, but
also by whether they preserve the facts needed for downstream reasoning.  On a
controlled clinical/legal benchmark, generic anonymization leaks identifiers and
quasi-identifiers, privacy-first anonymization often removes the answer, and a
small amount of task-aware structure improves the observed privacy--utility
tradeoff.  The stress slice further shows that some privacy--utility conflicts
are inherent: the sensitive fact may also be the task-critical fact.  This makes
task-critical utility evaluation a necessary complement to span-level privacy
metrics for sensitive text transformation.
